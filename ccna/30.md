## Wireless LAN Topologies

Wireless communication uses **radio frequency (RF)** signals to transmit data through open space. A **transmitter** sends these signals, and a **receiver** captures them. However, when multiple signals overlap, interference can occur. Because all devices share the same frequency channel, only one should transmit at a time.  

To manage this shared medium, devices that follow the **IEEE 802.11 standard** use a method called **Clear Channel Assessment (CCA)** to ensure the airwaves are free before sending data. This mechanism helps prevent collisions. As a result, **802.11 WLANs are always half-duplex**—meaning devices cannot send and receive simultaneously on the same frequency.

---

## Basic Service Set (BSS)

To maintain order in wireless environments, devices organize themselves into groups called **Basic Service Sets (BSS)**. Each BSS revolves around a **Wireless Access Point (AP)** operating in **infrastructure mode**, forming the foundation of the wireless network.

The AP broadcasts its presence using **beacon frames**, which include a **Service Set Identifier (SSID)**—the name of the wireless network. These beacons, sent roughly 10 times per second, also carry information about supported data rates and vendor-specific options. If an AP supports multiple SSIDs, it transmits multiple beacon frames, one per SSID.

Each AP has a unique **Basic Service Set Identifier (BSSID)** derived from its MAC address. Devices within range can learn about nearby BSSs by either:

- **Passive scanning:** Listening for beacon frames.  
- **Active scanning:** Sending **probe requests** and receiving **probe responses** from APs.

Once a device finds an AP, it can **associate** by exchanging 802.11 **association request** and **response** frames. Once connected, the device becomes a **station (STA)** within the BSS. Most communication from and to the client travels through the AP, keeping the network organized and controlled.

> ⚠️ Note: Wireless frames are broadcast over open air, not confined within cables. Unless encrypted, these frames can be intercepted by anyone within range.

---

## Distribution System (DS)

While an AP and its clients form a local wireless network, users often need access beyond their BSS. To provide this connectivity, the AP connects to a **wired Ethernet network**, known as the **Distribution System (DS)**. This system allows the AP to bridge wireless and wired communication.

Each SSID on the AP typically corresponds to a **VLAN**, requiring the AP to connect to the switch via a **trunk port** that carries multiple VLANs.

---

## Extended Service Set (ESS)

When multiple APs are deployed across larger areas, they can interconnect through the DS to form an **Extended Service Set (ESS)**.  

All APs in an ESS should broadcast the same **SSID**, allowing seamless mobility for clients. This shared SSID across APs is known as an **Extended Service Set Identifier (ESSID)**.  

Clients can move between APs through a process called **roaming**. When a client moves out of one AP’s range and into another’s, it sends a **reassociation request**, and the new AP responds with a **reassociation response**. Each AP operates on a distinct channel to avoid interference, so clients must scan for available channels during roaming.

---

## Independent Basic Service Set (IBSS)

Sometimes, wireless devices need to communicate directly without an AP. This configuration is known as an **Independent Basic Service Set (IBSS)** or **ad hoc mode**.  

In an IBSS, one device takes the lead by broadcasting the network name and parameters, while others join to form a peer-to-peer connection. This setup works well for temporary or small-scale wireless networking needs.

## Other Wireless Topologies

### Repeater
A **wireless repeater** extends signal coverage by receiving and retransmitting the signal.  
However, repeaters using a single radio for both tasks must operate on the same channel, which can reduce throughput by 50% due to repeated transmissions.  
Advanced repeaters use **dual radios**, each dedicated to separate channels, maintaining better performance.

---

### Workgroup Bridge (WGB)
A **Workgroup Bridge** connects wired devices to a wireless network, acting as a bridge between Ethernet and Wi-Fi.  
There are two main types:
- **Universal Workgroup Bridge (uWGB):** Connects a single wired device.  
- **Cisco Workgroup Bridge (WGB):** A proprietary version supporting multiple wired devices.

---

### Outdoor Bridge
An **Outdoor Bridge** extends network connectivity between distant LANs—commonly between buildings or sites—by creating a **point-to-point wireless link**. These links are ideal for long-range communication without physical cabling.

---

### Mesh Network
In a **mesh network**, multiple APs connect wirelessly to relay traffic in a chain-like structure.  
Mesh APs typically use **dual radios**:
- One radio maintains client access (BSS).  
- The other handles **backhaul traffic** between APs.  

At the network edge, mesh traffic connects back to the wired LAN. This design improves coverage and resilience in large or complex environments.

## Wireless Bands and Channels

Wi-Fi operates across several frequency bands:

| Band | Frequency Range | Notes |
|------|-----------------|-------|
| **2.4 GHz** | 2.400 – 2.4835 GHz | Longer range, better wall penetration, but more interference |
| **5 GHz** | 5.150 – 5.825 GHz | Higher throughput, more channels, shorter range |
| **6 GHz** | 5.925 – 7.125 GHz | Latest band with high efficiency and minimal interference |

The **2.4 GHz** band contains overlapping channels. To prevent interference, only **channels 1, 6, and 11** should be used simultaneously.  
In contrast, **5 GHz** and **6 GHz** bands consist entirely of **non-overlapping channels**, allowing greater capacity and performance.

## IEEE 802.11 Standards Overview

| Amendment | 2.4 GHz | 5 GHz | 6 GHz | Max Data Rate | Key Features |
|------------|:-------:|:------:|:------:|---------------|---------------|
| **802.11-1997** | ✅ | ❌ | ❌ | 2 Mbps | Original standard (1997) |
| **802.11b** | ✅ | ❌ | ❌ | 11 Mbps | Introduced in 1999 |
| **802.11a** | ❌ | ✅ | ❌ | 54 Mbps | Introduced in 1999 |
| **802.11g** | ✅ | ❌ | ❌ | 54 Mbps | Introduced in 2003 |
| **802.11n** | ✅ | ✅ | ❌ | 600 Mbps | High Throughput (HT, 2009) |
| **802.11ac** | ❌ | ✅ | ❌ | 6.93 Gbps | Very High Throughput (VHT, 2013) |
| **802.11ax (Wi-Fi 6)** | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | 4x 802.11n | High Efficiency (HE, latest standard) |

> **Note:** Devices must support the same standard to communicate, though many are backward compatible.

---

## Band Selection and Signal Propagation

Modern APs can operate on multiple bands simultaneously, allowing clients to connect on the best available one.  
Clients typically choose based on signal quality, driver settings, and operating system logic.  

In open environments, **2.4 GHz signals** travel farther and penetrate obstacles more effectively than **5 GHz** or **6 GHz** signals.  
However, **5 GHz and 6 GHz** provide higher performance and less interference—making them ideal for high-density environments.
