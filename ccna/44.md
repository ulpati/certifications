## Introduction to Quality of Service (QoS)

**Quality of Service (QoS)** refers to a collection of tools and mechanisms that allow network devices to handle data packets differently as they pass through. These mechanisms ensure that certain types of traffic receive the appropriate priority and performance based on their needs.

---

## Managing Bandwidth, Delay, Jitter, and Loss

Cisco devices—both routers and switches—provide multiple QoS features designed to manage four essential aspects of network performance:

| **Metric** | **Description** |
|-------------|----------------|
| **Bandwidth** | Represents the capacity or speed of a link — the number of bits that can be transmitted per second. QoS controls how bandwidth is shared among traffic types. |
| **Delay** | The time taken for a packet to travel from source to destination. Measured as **one-way** or **round-trip** delay. |
| **Jitter** | The variation in delay between consecutive packets. High jitter can disrupt real-time applications like voice and video. |
| **Loss** | The percentage of packets that never reach their destination, often due to congestion or errors. |

### Cisco’s Traffic Recommendations

| **Traffic Type** | **Delay (One-Way)** | **Jitter** | **Loss** |
|------------------|---------------------|-------------|-----------|
| **Voice (Interactive)** | ≤ 150 ms | ≤ 30 ms | ≤ 1% |
| **Video** | 200–400 ms | 30–50 ms | 0.1–1% |

---

## QoS on Switches and Routers

The fundamental QoS principles discussed here apply equally to both LAN switches and IP routers. The term *packet* is used broadly to describe any type of network message being processed.

---

## Classification and Marking

### What Are Classification and Marking?

**Classification** identifies packets based on their header values (such as IP address, protocol, or port number).  
**Marking** then alters specific bits in the packet headers to indicate the packet’s class or priority, enabling subsequent devices to apply the right QoS policies efficiently.

QoS features can operate on traffic entering or leaving an interface.

---

### Why Mark Packets?

Complex classification across many devices can be resource-intensive. Instead, networks typically perform **deep classification early** and **mark packets** with a priority value. Subsequent devices can then make simpler forwarding decisions based on these markings.

A common marking method uses the **Differentiated Services Code Point (DSCP)** field, a 6-bit section of the IP header dedicated to QoS.

---

### Classification Methods: ACLs and NBAR

Access Control Lists (ACLs) are commonly used for basic packet classification—if a packet matches an ACL’s *permit* rule, the QoS policy applies to that traffic.

For more advanced identification, Cisco provides **Network-Based Application Recognition (NBAR2)**, which inspects deeper into the packet to recognize applications not easily identified by port numbers alone (e.g., streaming apps or encrypted protocols).

---

## Marking Fields in Network Headers

### IP Header Marking

In IPv4, the **Type of Service (ToS)** byte originally included a 3-bit **IP Precedence (IPP)** field. Modern networks use the **DSCP** field (6 bits) instead, following the **Differentiated Services (DiffServ)** model.  
IPv6 includes a similar 6-bit DSCP field within its **Traffic Class** byte.

### Ethernet (802.1Q) Marking

In Ethernet frames using VLAN tags, the 802.1Q header includes a **3-bit priority field**, known as either **Class of Service (CoS)** or **Priority Code Point (PCP)**.

### Summary of QoS Marking Fields

| **Field Name** | **Header(s)** | **Bits** | **Usage Context** |
|----------------|---------------|-----------|--------------------|
| **DSCP** | IPv4 / IPv6 | 6 | End-to-end packet marking |
| **IPP** | IPv4 / IPv6 | 3 | Legacy IP precedence |
| **CoS (PCP)** | 802.1Q | 3 | VLAN trunks |
| **TID** | 802.11 | 3 | Wi-Fi frames |
| **EXP** | MPLS | 3 | MPLS WAN |

---

## Trust Boundaries

A **trust boundary** defines where within a network QoS markings can be trusted. Typically, markings from end-user devices are *not* trusted, while markings from internal network equipment (such as access switches) are accepted and propagated.

## DiffServ Recommended Marking Values

### Expedited Forwarding (EF)

- **DSCP Value:** 46  
- Designed for traffic requiring **low latency, jitter, and loss**—typically used for **voice**.

### Assured Forwarding (AF)

AF defines **four queues**, each with **three drop priorities**, allowing for finer control under congestion conditions.  
Notation format: `AFxy` — *x = queue number*, *y = drop precedence*.

| | **Best Drop** | **Medium Drop** | **Worst Drop** |
|--|---------------|----------------|----------------|
| **Queue 4 (High)** | AF41 (34) | AF42 (36) | AF43 (38) |
| **Queue 3** | AF31 (26) | AF32 (28) | AF33 (30) |
| **Queue 2** | AF21 (18) | AF22 (20) | AF23 (22) |
| **Queue 1 (Low)** | AF11 (10) | AF12 (12) | AF13 (14) |

### Class Selector (CS)

Backward-compatible DSCP values derived from legacy IP Precedence (IPP).

| **IPP** | **CS** | **DSCP (Decimal)** |
|----------|---------|--------------------|
| 0 | CS0 | 0 |
| 1 | CS1 | 8 |
| 2 | CS2 | 16 |
| 3 | CS3 | 24 |
| 4 | CS4 | 32 |
| 5 | CS5 | 40 |
| 6 | CS6 | 48 |
| 7 | CS7 | 56 |

## Queuing and Scheduling

When an interface is busy, outgoing packets are temporarily held in **queues**. The QoS system must decide **which packets to send first**.

### Round-Robin Scheduling

Round-robin schedulers cycle through queues sequentially, sending packets from each in turn. The **weighted** variant (WRR) allows higher-priority queues to send more data per cycle.

Cisco’s **Class-Based Weighted Fair Queuing (CBWFQ)** implements WRR logic, allowing administrators to guarantee a **minimum bandwidth** for each class of traffic.

### Low Latency Queuing (LLQ)

**LLQ** enhances CBWFQ by adding one or more **priority queues** for traffic that requires **minimal delay and jitter**, such as **voice and video**.  
To prevent lower-priority queues from starving, LLQ limits the maximum bandwidth available to the priority queue using **policing**.

If voice or video exceeds the limit, excess packets are dropped—hence, tools like **Call Admission Control (CAC)** are used to ensure the network doesn’t exceed configured capacity.

### Common Enterprise Prioritization Strategy

1. Use **CBWFQ** for general and non-interactive data.  
2. Allocate more bandwidth to **business-critical applications**.  
3. Apply **LLQ** to voice and video traffic.  
4. Separate **voice** and **video** into distinct queues.  
5. Configure bandwidth to prevent queue drops.  
6. Use **CAC** to control real-time session load.

## Shaping and Policing

These mechanisms are primarily used at **WAN edges** to control traffic entering or leaving the network.

### Traffic Shaping vs. Policing

Both monitor traffic rates against a configured limit:

| **Action** | **If Rate ≤ Limit** | **If Rate > Limit** |
|-------------|---------------------|----------------------|
| **Shaping** | Forward immediately | Delay (queue temporarily) |
| **Policing** | Forward immediately | Drop or re-mark packet |

---

### Policing

Policers compare actual traffic rates with a configured limit, allowing short bursts after idle periods.

They are typically deployed **at network edges**—for example, between two enterprise routers on a WAN link—to prevent one side from exceeding the contracted rate.  
If traffic exceeds limits, the provider may **re-mark packets** for lower priority rather than discarding them immediately.

Key traits:
- Measures traffic rate against threshold.  
- Allows temporary bursts.  
- Usually applied at **ingress**.  
- Can **drop** or **re-mark** packets.  

---

### Traffic Shaping

**Shaping** queues excess packets and releases them gradually at a steady rate, smoothing out traffic bursts. It’s often used before sending data to a service provider that enforces policing.

Shaping can also integrate with **CBWFQ** and **LLQ** to manage how queued packets are prioritized internally.

#### Choosing the Right Shaping Interval

A shaper alternates between sending and pausing to maintain the configured average rate.  
For **voice and video**, long pauses can introduce jitter.  
To avoid this, configure a **short shaping interval (≈10 ms)**, ensuring smoother traffic flow.

Key traits:
- Controls **egress traffic**.  
- Queues and releases packets at a controlled rate.  
- Works best alongside queue management tools.  

---

## Congestion Avoidance

When queues fill up, packets begin to drop—a situation known as **tail drop**. This can cause TCP sessions to slow down drastically as senders reduce their window sizes.  

**Congestion avoidance tools** aim to prevent tail drops by **intelligently discarding packets earlier**, signaling TCP senders to slow down before queues overflow.

These tools monitor average queue depth:
- Below **minimum threshold** → no packets dropped.  
- Between **min** and **max threshold** → drop small percentage.  
- Beyond **max threshold** → drop all packets (full drop).  

By doing so, they stabilize network performance and reduce mass retransmissions.
