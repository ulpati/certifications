## Understanding Campus LAN Topologies  

A **Campus LAN** refers to a local area network designed to connect users and devices within one or several nearby buildings. These networks are structured for scalability, reliability, and performance, using well-defined architectural layers.

---

### Two-Tier Campus Design (Collapsed Core)  

Cisco defines three types of switch roles in a typical LAN: **Access**, **Distribution**, and **Core**.  
In a **two-tier** (or *collapsed core*) design, the **access** and **distribution** layers handle all core functions, eliminating the need for separate core switches.

#### **Access Layer**
Access switches provide direct connectivity for end-user devices. They sit at the network’s edge and serve as the entry point for endpoint traffic. Common access-layer features include:

- Physical Ethernet connections for endpoints and wireless access points  
- **Power over Ethernet (PoE)** support  
- **Redundant uplinks** using EtherChannel and STP/RSTP  
- **Security controls** such as Port Security, DHCP Snooping, and Dynamic ARP Inspection (DAI)  
- **QoS trust boundaries** for traffic prioritization  

#### **Distribution Layer**
Distribution switches connect multiple access switches and link them to the wider network or WAN. Typical functions include:

- Aggregating access layer connections  
- Providing inter-VLAN routing and default gateways  
- Implementing routing protocols and redundancy (via FHRPs)  
- Load balancing across redundant paths  
- Applying QoS and Access Control Lists (ACLs)  

A two-tier design ensures:  
1. End-user devices have local access through the access layer.  
2. Efficient connectivity between devices via the distribution layer with minimal cabling complexity.  

---

### Three-Tier Campus Design (Core Layer)  

A **three-tier design** adds **core switches** between the distribution layer and the rest of the enterprise or WAN. The **core layer** focuses purely on high-speed forwarding and reliability.

#### **Core Layer Key Functions**
- High-speed interconnection between distribution switches  
- Low latency and minimal delay  
- Redundant links for fault tolerance  
- Exclusion of unnecessary services (to maximize speed and stability)  

#### **Summary of Switch Roles**
| Layer | Primary Role | Key Features |
|-------|---------------|---------------|
| **Access** | Connects end devices | PoE, port security, DHCP Snooping |
| **Distribution** | Aggregates access switches | Routing, ACLs, redundancy |
| **Core** | Connects distribution switches | High-speed, low-latency transport |

---

### Topology Design Terms  

- **Star topology:** A single access switch connects endpoints directly.  
- **Partial mesh:** Some nodes interconnect but not all.  
- **Full mesh:** Every device connects directly to all others (rare in LANs due to cost and complexity).  

A full mesh is inefficient for campus LANs because most traffic flows between endpoints and servers—not between endpoints themselves.

## Ethernet Physical Media and Standards  

Campus networks use **Ethernet** at the data-link layer but may vary at the physical layer based on distance, bandwidth, and media type.

---

### Ethernet UTP Links at the Access Layer  

Most access-layer connections use **unshielded twisted pair (UTP)** cabling.

| Standard | Minimum Cable Category | Maximum Speed |
|-----------|------------------------|----------------|
| 10BASE-T | CAT 3 | 10 Mbps |
| 100BASE-T | CAT 5 | 100 Mbps |
| 1000BASE-T | CAT 5e | 1 Gbps |
| 2.5GBASE-T | CAT 5e | 2.5 Gbps |
| 5GBASE-T | CAT 5e | 5 Gbps |
| 10GBASE-T | CAT 6A (or CAT 6 up to 55 m) | 10 Gbps |
| 40GBASE-T | CAT 8 | 40 Gbps |

---

### Multigig Ethernet (2.5G/5G on CAT 5e)  

To avoid expensive cable upgrades, IEEE introduced **Multigig Ethernet**, supporting 2.5 Gbps and 5 Gbps speeds over existing CAT 5e cabling.  

**Multigig devices** (NICs and switch ports) use **autonegotiation** to select the highest supported speed.

---

### Fiber Uplinks  

For uplink connections—especially over long distances—**fiber optic cables** are preferred.  
Fiber categories are defined by **Optical Multimode (OM)** ratings:

| OM Category | 1000BASE-XS Distance | 10GBASE-SR Distance |
|--------------|----------------------|----------------------|
| OM1 | 220 m | 33 m |
| OM2 | 550 m | 82 m |
| OM3 | N/A | 300 m |
| OM4 | N/A | 400 m |

When upgrading, verify the OM category to ensure compatibility with target speeds.

### Small Office / Home Office (SOHO) Networks  

A **wireless router** in a home or small office combines multiple enterprise-level roles:  
- **Switch:** Handles wired connections  
- **Access Point:** Manages Wi-Fi communication  
- **Router:** Directs IP traffic between LAN and WAN  
- **Firewall:** Secures inbound and outbound connections

## Power over Ethernet (PoE)  

**PoE** technology allows devices to receive both data and power through a single Ethernet cable—eliminating the need for separate power sources.

### How PoE Works  

- **Power Sourcing Equipment (PSE):** Usually a LAN switch that delivers DC power  
- **Powered Device (PD):** The device receiving power (e.g., access points, IP phones, cameras)  

PoE is ideal for installations where running additional power lines is impractical. It also provides centralized power control and monitoring.

---

### PoE Operation Overview  

PoE ensures safety by performing **power detection** and **classification** before sending power.

1. **Detection:**  
   - The switch sends a low-voltage probe to detect whether the connected device is PoE-capable.  
   - If a valid resistance signature is found, it confirms a PD is present.  

2. **Classification:**  
   - Determines how much power to supply.  
   - **Layer 1 classification:** Uses electrical signals to assign a basic power class.  
   - **Layer 2 classification:** Uses **LLDP** or **CDP** to negotiate exact power needs.  

#### **Power Classes**

| Name | Power (at PSE) | Class | Powered Pairs |
|------|----------------|-------|----------------|
| PoE | 15 W | 0 | 2 |
| PoE+ | 30 W | 4 | 2 |
| UPoE | 60 W | 6 | 4 |
| UPoE+ | 90 W | 8 | 4 |

---

### PoE Design Considerations  

When designing a PoE-enabled LAN, consider the following:  
- **Device Power Requirements:** Identify all PD types and their wattage.  
- **Power Budget:** Calculate the total power needs for each switch and wiring closet.  
- **Switch Capability:** Ensure switches support the required PoE standard (PoE, PoE+, UPoE, etc.).  
- **Power Supplies:** Choose power supplies that meet or exceed total wattage demands.  
- **Efficiency Planning:** Avoid over-reserving power if devices require significantly less than their PoE class maximum.
