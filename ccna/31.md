## Autonomous Access Point (AP) Architecture

An **autonomous access point** operates as a **self-contained wireless device**. It includes both wired and wireless interfaces, allowing it to bridge traffic between wireless clients and the wired network directly.  

Because each autonomous AP works independently, traffic between wireless and wired networks travels only through the AP itself—creating a **short and efficient data path**. Two clients connected to the same AP can even communicate directly without involving the wired network.

However, since each AP must be configured manually, managing large deployments can become complex. Tools such as **Cisco Prime Infrastructure** or **Cisco Catalyst Center** can simplify centralized management, but network scalability remains a challenge as configurations and VLANs must reach every AP individually.

## Cloud-Based AP Architecture

In a **cloud-managed architecture**, AP management is offloaded to a **cloud service** rather than handled within the enterprise network.  

Through a web-based management platform, administrators can configure APs, monitor performance, track wireless activity, and generate reports.  

Importantly, user traffic **does not traverse the cloud**—the cloud handles only management and configuration. The architecture is divided into two logical planes:

- **Control Plane:** Manages configuration, monitoring, and operational commands.  
- **Data Plane:** Carries user traffic between wireless clients and the wired network.  

This separation keeps data paths short and management centralized, improving scalability and visibility.

## Split-MAC Architecture

Cisco’s **Split-MAC architecture** divides access point functions between the **AP** and a centralized **Wireless LAN Controller (WLC)**.  

### Management Functions (handled by the WLC)
- RF management  
- Roaming and association control  
- Authentication and security enforcement  
- Quality of Service (QoS) policies  

### Real-Time Functions (handled by the AP)
- Transmitting and receiving RF signals  
- MAC layer operations  
- Data encryption and decryption  

The AP maintains low-level communication with clients while the WLC oversees higher-level coordination.

This division enables **lightweight APs (LWAPs)** that rely on the WLC for centralized management. Communication between APs and the controller occurs over a **CAPWAP (Control and Provisioning of Wireless Access Points)** tunnel.

### CAPWAP Communication Channels
- **Control Messages (UDP 5246):** Used for configuration and management. These are encrypted and authenticated for security.  
- **Data Messages (UDP 5247):** Carry user traffic between APs and the WLC. Data is encapsulated but not encrypted unless DTLS encryption is enabled.

Each AP and WLC uses **preinstalled X.509 digital certificates** for mutual authentication, ensuring only authorized devices join the network.  

Because CAPWAP encapsulates traffic as IP packets, an AP only needs **one IP address** for both management and tunneling—no VLAN trunking is required.

## Wireless LAN Controller (WLC) Features

Once the WLC is managing APs, it can provide additional capabilities:

- **Dynamic Channel Assignment:** Automatically selects optimal RF channels.  
- **Transmit Power Control:** Adjusts AP power to maintain consistent coverage.  
- **Self-Healing Coverage:** Nearby APs increase power if another AP fails.  
- **Fast Roaming:** Enables seamless client movement between APs.  
- **Load Balancing:** Distributes clients evenly across APs.  
- **RF Monitoring:** Detects interference, noise, rogue APs, and ad hoc devices.  
- **Centralized Security:** Authenticates clients and enforces trusted DHCP use.  
- **Wireless Intrusion Prevention:** Detects and mitigates malicious wireless activity.

## Comparing Cisco Wireless LAN Controller Deployments

Different deployment models offer flexibility for various environments:

| **Deployment Model** | **WLC Location** | **APs Supported** | **Clients Supported** | **Typical Use Case** |
|------------------------|------------------|-------------------|-----------------------|----------------------|
| **Centralized** | Data center | ~6000 | ~64,000 | Large enterprise or campus |
| **Cloud-Based** | Public or private cloud | ~6000 | ~64,000 | Enterprise with distributed sites |
| **Distributed** | Access layer | ~250 | ~5,000 | Small campus or multi-building site |
| **Embedded** | Co-located on AP | ~100 | ~2,000 | Branch offices or small sites |
| **Autonomous** | None | N/A | N/A | Standalone APs without controller |

---

### Centralized Deployment
A **centralized WLC** resides in a data center, managing thousands of APs. It’s ideal for enterprises where resources (servers, storage, internet access) are also centralized. Policies and configurations are unified, simplifying control and security enforcement.

---

### Cloud-Based Deployment
When the WLC operates within a **cloud environment**, either private or public, it can manage distributed APs remotely.  

In a **private cloud**, latency between APs and WLCs remains minimal. In a **public cloud**, CAPWAP control tunnels connect APs to the WLC, but user data is **locally switched** at the AP level using **FlexConnect mode**, reducing the dependency on long data paths.

---

### Distributed Deployment
A **distributed WLC** model positions multiple controllers within the network, closer to APs. This setup suits **medium-sized campuses** where each WLC manages a smaller number of APs (typically up to 250).

---

### Embedded Wireless Controller (EWC)
In smaller environments, a **controller-less model** can be deployed using an **Embedded Wireless Controller** built into one of the APs.  
This AP acts as both a controller and an access point, managing other APs locally through CAPWAP tunnels. An EWC typically supports up to 100 APs and 2,000 clients—perfect for **branch offices** or **retail sites**.

## Cisco AP Operational Modes

Cisco access points support several modes depending on deployment needs:

| **Mode** | **Description** |
|-----------|----------------|
| **Local** | Default lightweight mode; provides service on a specific channel and scans others for interference or rogue devices. |
| **Monitor** | Disables transmission but listens for rogue APs and wireless intrusion events. |
| **FlexConnect** | Allows local traffic switching even when disconnected from the WLC. |
| **Sniffer** | Captures 802.11 frames for analysis tools like *Wireshark*. |
| **Rogue Detector** | Correlates wired and wireless MAC addresses to detect rogue devices. |
| **Bridge** | Operates as a wireless bridge, linking two or more networks (useful for mesh networks). |
| **Flex+Bridge** | Combines FlexConnect and Bridge functions for mesh deployments. |
| **SE-Connect** | Performs full-spectrum analysis for interference detection (e.g., using *Cisco Spectrum Expert*). |

---

## FlexConnect Mode

**FlexConnect** allows APs at remote sites to continue operating if their connection to the WLC fails.  

In **connected mode**, the AP sends all traffic through the CAPWAP tunnel. If the **WAN link goes down**, the AP automatically enters **standalone mode**, maintaining local connectivity for wireless clients.  

Administrators can also configure **local switching** for specific SSIDs—even when the WLC is reachable—to optimize performance and prevent unnecessary routing through the controller.

| **Feature** | **FlexConnect** | **OfficeExtend** |
|--------------|-----------------|------------------|
| AP joins WLC | Yes | Yes |
| Data traffic encryption | Optional | Yes (DTLS) |
| Typical APs per site | Unlimited | One |
| Roaming support | Yes | No |
